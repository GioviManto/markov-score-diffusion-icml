Diffusion models are trained to approximate the score of a noised data distribution,
a target that is unknown for almost every distribution of interest. We study a family
in which it is known exactly and non-trivial: a first-order Markov chain with
continuous, non-Gaussian transitions under coordinatewise Ornstein--Uhlenbeck noise,
where conditioning leaves the posterior factor graph a tree and one forward--backward
sweep of functional message passing returns the exact score. The laboratory separates
what diffusion noise does to different orders of structure. The channel attenuates the
Hermite moment of degree $m$ by $e^{-mt}$ exactly, so that, for a law with an
exponential moment, the Fisher information one noised sequence carries about a
feature first visible at degree $m$ decays as $e^{-2mt}$ with an explicit constant:
correlation (degree two) as $e^{-4t}$, non-Gaussian shape (degree four) as
$e^{-8t}$; and the gap between the true score and that of the covariance-matched
Gaussian---which we show is exactly what Gaussian message closure computes---is
$\tfrac16\lVert\kappa_4\rVert_F^2e^{-8t}$, with the crossover set by the covariance
spectrum. Tree-exact inference verifies the rates and the constants, adding the
fourth-order term back to the Gaussian score closes the gap wherever the expansion
converges, and heavy-tailed innovations reach the same constant. We then learn the
transition law from noised sequences alone by expectation--maximisation around the same
recursion: the fitted shape sits at the information reference while the coordinate is
identifiable and collapses to a Gaussian innovation where the reference says it must,
and moment estimators show the predicted rates at a fraction of the efficiency.
Against six sequence denoisers trained under one recipe the structured estimator is
ahead at every sample size; at the estimator's own information interface---one noised
view per sequence, no clean chain, no true score for model selection---the best network
is a factor $\matchedRatioBestD$ behind. The advantage is earned by the non-Gaussian
capacity of the innovation model, transfers to generation, extrapolates in length, and
ends at a misspecification strength that a closed-form bias predicts.
