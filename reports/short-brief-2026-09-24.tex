\section*{1.\quad What we are doing, and why this way}

\subsection{The problem} A diffusion model generates data by learning to reverse a noising
process. To reverse it, it must learn one object at every noise level: the \emph{score}, the
direction in which noisy data becomes more likely. On real data nobody knows the true score, so
nobody can measure how far a learned one is from it --- progress is judged by proxies such as
sample quality. That leaves a class of questions unanswerable. How much information does the noise
actually destroy? What kind of structure is lost first? And does knowing something about how the
data is built help, or is a large enough network always enough?

\subsection{The trick} We work where the true score can be computed exactly, so those questions
have exact answers. The data comes from a Markov chain: a sequence $a_1,\dots,a_{\nsites}$ in which
each site is a fraction of the previous one plus fresh randomness,
\[
a_{i+1} \;=\; \corr\,a_i + \varepsilon_i .
\]
Because the dependencies form a chain, the exact score of the \emph{noised} sequence can be
computed by message passing, to numerical precision. Every error reported below is measured against
that truth rather than against a proxy.

\subsection{The two knobs} $\corr$ is the correlation: how strongly each site remembers the last.
The innovation $\varepsilon$ is drawn from a \emph{generalised Gaussian}, whose density falls off
like $e^{-|x/s|^{\beta}}$. The exponent $\beta$ is the \textbf{shape}: it sets how the randomness is
distributed at a fixed spread. The scale $s$ is always chosen so the variance is identical for every
$\beta$, so $\beta$ moves the \emph{fourth} moment while the second stays put --- which is exactly
what separates ``non-Gaussian structure'' from ``more noise''.

\subsection{The comparison} Two ways of learning the score are put side by side. Ours knows the
data is a chain, and fits the chain's parameters by EM with exact message passing. The competitors
are neural networks --- transformers and convolutional window models --- that know nothing about
the structure. Both are shown \emph{exactly the same information}: one noisy view of each sequence,
never the clean one, and neither may consult the true score to decide when to stop training. That
last condition is the one comparisons of this kind most often violate.

\subsection{How we measure it} Every number in this document is a \emph{relative score error}:
the size of the gap between a method's score and the true one, divided by the size of the true
score itself, pooled over twelve noise levels. It has three natural landmarks. The exact score
scores $0$. Ignoring the data's structure entirely --- answering as if the sites were independent
--- scores $\isoFloorLaplace$. And $\lmmseFloorLaplace$ is the best score achievable by any method
that captures correlations but nothing beyond them; we prove that this is a genuine floor, not
merely the best we managed, which is what makes it possible to say what a method has and has not
learned rather than only which method won.

\vspace{-0.6em}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Noise destroys fine structure far faster than correlation, at an exact rate} This is
the theoretical result, and panel (a) is it measured. Structure of each order decays at its own
exponential rate under the noise, so the higher-order detail that makes data non-Gaussian vanishes
first while plain correlation survives much longer. The two exponents differ by a factor of two,
and the level at which they cross is a formula rather than a fitted curve. The practical
consequence: above a computable noise level, everything a model can still learn about the data is
second-order --- and we can say where that level is.

\subsection{Knowing the structure is worth 5--10$\times$ at equal information} Our estimator is
ahead in all six regimes and in every one of the sixteen repetitions of each. The comparison is
deliberately unflattering to us: the networks are re-screened and re-tuned separately inside each
regime, while our method is denied the true score when choosing where to stop and selects on
held-out evidence instead. The advantage is \emph{smallest} where the chain carries the most
non-Gaussian structure --- the direction panel (a) predicts, so the experiment and the theory agree
on a detail neither was fitted to.

\subsection{The advantage is fourth-order structure, and nothing else} The capacity experiment is
the control. Restricted to a single Gaussian innovation, the chain model lands exactly on the
second-order floor ($\lmmseFloorLaplace$) and stays there however much data it is given; allowed
richer innovations it goes far below. The only difference between those two arms is the freedom to
represent non-Gaussian structure, so that is what the margin is made of. The same holds across
fifteen further combinations of $(\corr,\nsites)$, with the restricted arm matching the theoretical
floor to $\regIdentMax$ in the worst of them --- which incidentally tests the grid, the message
passing, the EM and the scoring simultaneously. It also holds with the non-Gaussianity reversed, on
a chain \emph{less} heavy-tailed than Gaussian: the factor there is
$\rbBimodalIsmRatioEveightfivethirtytwo$
[$\rbBimodalIsmRatioEveightfivethirtytwolo$, $\rbBimodalIsmRatioEveightfivethirtytwohi$].

\section*{3.\quad Where it stops working, and what the evidence rests on}

\begin{figure}[H]
\vspace{-0.2em}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig-boundary.pdf}
\caption{How wrong the first-order chain assumption may be before the advantage goes. Each panel
pushes the data away from the assumed model in a different way and plots a network's error divided
by ours; above the dotted line at $1$ the misspecified chain estimator still wins. Every
perturbation is Gaussian, so the true score of the \emph{perturbed} model remains exactly

assumption.}
\vspace{-0.4em}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The boundary} Two departures reverse the ordering inside the range swept: adding a
second order of memory, at a coefficient between $\misBreakLoArtwoD$ and $\misBreakHiArtwoD$, and
letting the correlation vary along the sequence, between $\misBreakLoSitevarD$ and
$\misBreakHiSitevarD$. Two others never reverse it --- a rank-one global latent is absorbed into
the fitted correlation, and a long-range coupling decays to a tie. The two sample sizes also
separate the two ways of being wrong: at zero perturbation the error halves when the data
quadruples, which is estimation error; past break-even it does not move at all, which is bias, and
no amount of data will fix it.

\subsection{What the numbers rest on} Every experiment behind this page is frozen: its outputs are
hash-pinned and its configuration was registered before it ran. Each number here is generated from
those outputs by a tool and then recomputed independently by a second tool that fails if the two
disagree, and the figures check themselves against the manuscript's own numbers and refuse to be
written if a value does not match. One experiment, a sample-complexity measurement, is still
running on the cluster; nothing on this page depends on it.

\subsection{One correction on the record} The capacity sweep in the earlier archival study is void.
Its training set was drawn in a way that re-created the random generator for each sequence, so every
configuration was in fact fitted to a single repeated sequence. The earlier explanation offered for
that study's conclusion --- a stopping-rule asymmetry --- is withdrawn with it. The capacity
evidence above was computed independently and is unaffected.
