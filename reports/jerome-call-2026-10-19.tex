\section*{In one page}

\textbf{What we did.} We built the second laboratory of your email: exact snapshots of the one-dimensional $\phi^4$
(stochastic Allen--Cahn) field, six settings across temperature and lattice spacing, and the four arms you proposed (EM with a
learned pair energy, the Gaussian closure, a window CNN, the exact score), plus a transformer, a neural (MDN) kernel and the
paper's own mixture kernel. EM--BP learns from noisy snapshots only; the networks are trained both on noisy snapshots and,
with more information, on clean ones. Sixteen seeds; $\nseq=32$ to $2048$ sequences (networks at $128$ and $2048$). Each
experiment was registered with its predictions before the main run, and its outputs are frozen by checksum. Predictions that
failed are reported as such (\fals{in italics}).

\textbf{What came out.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{The five-parameter site energy wins everywhere.} Your pair energy, a Gaussian bond plus a quartic potential per site, learned by EM--BP
through the noise, is the best arm on every cell at every $\nseq$ (on average over seeds). At $\nseq=2048$ its error is
$\fldSiteFloorMin$--$\fldSiteFloorMax$ of the Gaussian closure's, and the best network is $\fldNetRatioMin$--$\fldNetRatioMax$
times worse.
\item \textbf{Its parameters are the physics.} The fitted temperature is within $10\%$ of the truth in $\fldThetaOkFits$ of
$\fldThetaFits$ fits, the barrier height within $20\%$ in $\fldBarrierOkFits$ of $\fldBarrierFits$, and the learned potential
is the double well (Figure~\ref{fig:potential}). Samples drawn from it have the right kinks and domain lengths.
\item \textbf{The kernel family is the modelling choice.} The paper's mixture kernel, whose expected next value is linear in the
current one, learns nothing beyond the Gaussian closure ($\fldMixFloorMin$--$\fldMixFloorMax$ times its error). A neural kernel
earns its parameters only where the simple family is wrong (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\item \textbf{Your refinement prediction holds.} As $\latt$ goes from $1$ to $\tfrac14$ at fixed physical length, the window CNN
goes from $\fldWindowFloorHone$ to $\fldWindowFloorHzeroptwofive$ times the Gaussian closure's error (its radius re-chosen on
each lattice), while the site energy stays at $3$--$4\%$ of it.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{What is weaker than it sounds} (Section~\ref{sec:honest}). BP is slower per sequence than the networks; the stopping
point of every arm, networks included, is chosen with the exact score on a validation set (a likelihood-based rule gives
$4$--$23\%$ instead of $3$--$17\%$); with $\nseq=32$ on the two hottest cells the site energy is still above the Gaussian
closure; several neural-kernel numbers are upper bounds.

\section{The field as a Markov chain}
\label{sec:model}

The SPDE and its stationary law (we write $\Temp$ for the temperature, because $T$ is the transfer operator):
\[
\partial_\tau\phi=\partial_x^2\phi-V'(\phi)+\sqrt{2\Temp}\,\eta,\qquad V(\phi)=\tfrac14(\phi^2-1)^2,\qquad
p[\phi]\propto\exp\Big(-\tfrac1\Temp\textstyle\int\big[\tfrac12(\partial_x\phi)^2+V(\phi)\big]\dd x\Big).
\]
On a lattice of spacing $\latt$ the energy couples only neighbours,
\[
p(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_\nsites)\;\propto\;\prod_i\exp\Big(-\frac{(\phi_{i+1}-\phi_i)^2}{2\Temp\latt}-\frac{\latt\,V(\phi_i)}{\Temp}\Big),
\]
so a snapshot is exactly a Markov chain in the site index. With the symmetric transfer operator
$\transfer(\phi,\phi')=e^{-\latt V(\phi)/2\Temp}\,e^{-(\phi'-\phi)^2/2\Temp\latt}\,e^{-\latt V(\phi')/2\Temp}$, its Perron
pair $\transfer\psi_0=\lambda_0\psi_0$, the stationary chain has
\[
\kernel(\phi'\mid\phi)=\frac{\transfer(\phi,\phi')\,\psi_0(\phi')}{\lambda_0\,\psi_0(\phi)}
=\frac{\normal{\phi';\phi}{\Temp\latt}\,g(\phi')}{Z(\phi)},\qquad g=\psi_0\,e^{-\latt V/2\Temp},\qquad \pi=\psi_0^2 .
\]
The second form is your ground-state transform written as a \emph{tilted walk}: a Gaussian step re-weighted by a function of the
arrival point, with $Z$ its normaliser. Two facts follow and matter below:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The drift is non-linear.} In standardised units $a=\phi/s$ the step variance is $\bondvar=\Temp\latt/s^2$, and
differentiating $Z$ gives $\E[a'-a\mid a]=\bondvar\,(\log Z)'(a)$. The paper's
mixture kernel $a'=\corr a+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\sum_c w_c\normal{\mu_c}{\sigma_c^2}$, has $\E[a'\mid a]=\corr a+$const,
so it cannot hold a site inside a domain. The two families meet only at the Gaussian linear chain.
\item \textbf{Correlation length} $\corrlen=1/\log(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)$ in sites; it grows like $1/\latt$ under refinement and
roughly like $e^{E_{\mathrm{kink}}/\Temp}$ as the field cools. There is no transition at any $\Temp>0$.
\end{itemize}

\section{The estimator: your pair energy, learned through the noise}
\label{sec:estimator}

\textbf{Score from BP.} Data are corrupted as $\vx=e^{-t}\va+\sqrt{\Dt}\,\vz$, $\Dt=1-e^{-2t}$. By Tweedie,
$\nabla_\vx\log\Pt(\vx)=-(\vx-e^{-t}\E[\va\mid\vx])/\Dt$, and on a chain the posterior mean is exact by one forward and one
backward BP sweep through $\kernel$, with messages on a 401-point grid on $[-8,8]$.

\textbf{The model.} On the standardised field the pair energy is a Gaussian bond plus a site energy,
\[
W_\params(a,a')=\frac{(a'-a)^2}{2\bondvar}+f_\params(a)+f_\params(a'),\qquad f_\params(a)=\sum_{m=1}^{4}c_m\Big(\frac a2\Big)^m,
\qquad \params=(\log\bondvar,c_1,\dots,c_4),
\]
five parameters. We call this arm the \emph{site energy}, after its free function $f_\params$; it is the pair energy of your
email. The kernel is the ground-state transform of
$\transfer_\params(a,a')=e^{-f_\params(a)}\,\normal{a';a}{\bondvar}\,e^{-f_\params(a')}$ (one eigensolve per evaluation). The truth
is in the family: $\bondvar=\Temp\latt/s^2$ and $f=(\latt/2\Temp)\,V(sa)+$const.

\textbf{Learning = Boltzmann learning with a posterior.} For one noisy sequence, Fisher's identity gives
\[
\nabla_\params\log p_\params(\vx)=\E_{p_\params(\va\mid\vx)}\Big[\nabla_\params\log p_\params(\va)\Big]
=-\,\E_{\text{posterior}}\Big[\textstyle\sum_i\nabla_\params W_\params(a_i,a_{i+1})\Big]
+\E_{\text{model}}\Big[\textstyle\sum_i\nabla_\params W_\params(a_i,a_{i+1})\Big],
\]
for the Gibbs form $p_\params(\va)\propto\exp(-\sum_iW_\params(a_i,a_{i+1}))$: your $\E_{\rm data}[\nabla W]-\E_{\rm model}[\nabla W]$
with the data term replaced by a posterior expectation. EM does this
in two steps: the E-step runs BP on each noisy sequence and accumulates the expected pair counts $\Xis(a,a')$; the M-step
increases $\Qfun(\params)=\langle\Xis,\log\kernelth\rangle$ (L-BFGS, gradient through the perturbation theory of the Perron
vector; not concave, so this is generalised EM). EM's stopping point is chosen on a validation set (Section~\ref{sec:honest}).

\textbf{Directed or symmetric? The same class.} Any tilted walk
$\kernel_g(a'\mid a)=\normal{a';a}{\bondvar}\,g(a')/Z_g(a)$ is reversible, with invariant law $\pi_g\propto g\,Z_g$, because
$\pi_g(a)\kernel_g(a'\mid a)\propto g(a)\,g(a')\,\normal{a';a}{\bondvar}$ is symmetric. It is the Gibbs chain of the pair energy
above with $f=-\tfrac12\log(g/Z_g)$. So the choice is only about parametrisation: a flexible tilt $\log g$ (we run a spline,
$25$ numbers, and a Legendre polynomial, $7$) or the site energy itself ($5$, a quartic for the field). A fitted kernel then
reads as physics: $\hat\Temp=\hat\bondvar s^2/\latt$ and $\hat V(\phi)=(2\hat\Temp/\latt)\hat f(\phi/s)+$const.

\textbf{What the Gaussian closure is here.}
\label{sec:closure}
Forcing the messages to be Gaussian (moment projection, which on a linear chain is exactly the Kalman smoother) is not a usable
estimator on the field: far from the bulk the kernel jumps back towards it almost regardless of where it starts, so the
backward messages are nearly flat across the grid and their projection discards them ($\fldProjEdgeBase$ of a projected
message's mass in the outer $5\%$ of the grid on the base cell, $\fldProjEdgeOthersMin$ or more on the others). The Gaussian arm
is therefore the covariance twin's exact answer, the best linear estimator, computed in closed form: the ``linear floor''.

\textbf{Arms.} EM--BP with five kernels (site energy 5, Legendre tilt 7, spline tilt 25, paper's mixture 25, MDN 1{,}516);
window CNN and transformer, each trained two ways, on one noisy copy per sequence (ISM, EM--BP's own information) and on clean
targets with one fixed corruption (DSM, strictly more information); the linear floor; the exact oracle. The error is
$R=\big(\sum\|\hat\score-\score\|^2/\sum\|\score\|^2\big)^{1/2}$, pooled over test chains, sites and twelve levels
$t\in[0.05,3]$; tables report $R$ divided by the linear floor, so $1$ is the Gaussian closure.

\section{Your predictions: refinement, cost, speciation}
\label{sec:predictions}

\textbf{Refinement.} The correlation length in sites grows like $1/\latt$ ($\fldXiHone$ to $\fldXiHzeroptwofive$ from $\latt=1$ to
$\tfrac14$). The window CNN's radius was screened on each lattice and the screen picked $2$, $4$, $8$ sites, a constant
physical reach ($8$ was the largest offered). Trained on noisy copies (ISM) with $\nseq=2048$, its absolute error falls ($\fldWindowRiskHone\to\fldWindowRiskHzeroptwofive$), but
relative to the spline it grows from $\fldWindowSplineHone$ to $\fldWindowSplineHzeroptwofive$, and relative to the floor from
$\fldWindowFloorHone$ to $\fldWindowFloorHzeroptwofive$: on the finest lattice the window is worse than the Gaussian closure.
Per step the field becomes nearly Gaussian as $\latt\to0$ (your $O(\latt)$ caveat), the floor falls ($0.269\to0.098$), and the
site energy still stays at $3$--$4\%$ of it: the structure builds up over many sites, and BP collects it. Whether a wider
window closes the gap was not tested.

\textbf{Cost.} Figure~\ref{fig:runtime}: the networks own the cheap end ($\fldNetCostMin$--$\fldNetCostMax$\,ms per sequence) and

against $\cmpBpMsOneThread$\,ms at $401$. The paper's mixtures need about $151$ points, because with much data they learn
components narrower than a $101$-point spacing.

\textbf{Speciation.} For a noisy snapshot the exact posterior gives each site $p_i=P(a_i>0\mid\vx)$; the commitment
$|2p_i-1|$ goes from $0$ (both wells equally likely) to $1$ (the site has chosen), and $t_c$ is the level where its site
average crosses $\tfrac12$. All five registered predictions on commitment held (Figure~\ref{fig:speciation}):
\begin{itemize}
\item the posterior is calibrated: the share of signs guessed right equals $(1+\text{commitment})/2$ to $\fldSpecCalibDev$;
\item colder fields commit earlier in the reverse process: $t_c$ falls from $\fldSpecTcCold$ at $\Temp=0.2$ to $\fldSpecTcHot$ at
$\Temp=2$;
\item the exact posterior commits before its Gaussian twin at every temperature, by $\fldSpecExcessCold$ in $t$ when coldest
and $\fldSpecExcessHot$ when hottest. At $\Temp=0.2$ it is half-committed when the noise variance is $\fldSpecNoiseRatioExact$ times
the signal's, the twin only at $\fldSpecNoiseRatioTwin$: the two humps, not just the correlations, decide when it commits;
\item $t_c$ grows with the chain length ($\fldSpecTcLfour$ at $\nsites=4$, $\fldSpecTcLsixtyfour$ at $64$) and stops once the
chain is several domains long ($\fldSpecTcLonetwentyeight$, $\fldSpecTcLtwofiftysix$ at $128$, $256$);
\item below the correlation length, $t_c$ moves one-for-one with $\tfrac12\ln(1+\lambda_{\max})$ (slope $\fldSpecSlope$),
$\lambda_{\max}$ the largest covariance eigenvalue: the finite-chain analogue of the speciation time $\tfrac12\ln\lambda_{\max}$ of
Biroli, Bonnaire, de~Bortoli and Mézard.
\end{itemize}
\fals{The dilute-kink law $\corrlen\propto\Temp^{1/2}e^{E_{\mathrm{kink}}/\Temp}$, fitted on $\Temp\in[0.1,0.3]$, returns a barrier
of $\fldKinkFit$ against $2\sqrt2/3$ (predicted within $2\%$).} The local slope approaches the theory as $\Temp$ falls, so the
law looks right but these temperatures do not pin its constant; the lattice itself is converged ($\corrlen$ moves by
$\fldKinkConvergence$ from $\latt=1/16$ to $1/32$).

\section{What we do not claim}
\label{sec:honest}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Speed.} BP is slower per sequence than a network. On the 401-point grid with eight threads it is
$\cmpRatioCpu\times$ the transformer on CPU and $\cmpRatioGpu\times$ on an H200. On one thread the gap narrows ($3.2\times$ at
$401$ points) and at $101$ points BP is faster than the transformer, but still slower than the GRU. The networks timed are the
largest screened variants.
\item \textbf{Stopping rule.} Every arm's stopping point or checkpoint, networks included, is picked with the exact score on a

\section{The 25 September questions}
\label{sec:questions}

Answered in the order of the call. The linear chain is $a_{i+1}=\corr a_i+\varepsilon_i$, $\corr=0.85$, Laplace driving noise,
$\nsites=32$, unless stated.

\textbf{Q1. Gaussian messages = Gaussian score = Kalman?} Yes. On a linear chain, BP whose messages are projected onto Gaussians
returns exactly the best linear estimator of the covariance-matched Gaussian chain,
$\lmmse(\vx)=e^{-t}\Sig(e^{-2t}\Sig+\Dt\Id)^{-1}\vx$, which is the Rauch--Tung--Striebel smoother. The hypothesis that matters is
that the first message is Gaussian (or projected) too; without it the claim is false, by $4\times10^{-2}$ at the first site in a
computed counterexample. Check: grid
BP, the closed form and an independent RTS smoother agree to $1.9\times10^{-13}$. \fals{Projected BP run on the same 401-point
grid is off by up to $0.26$: its backward messages widen past the grid's edge.} Not new: the thesis had it; the paper now
says ``Kalman''.

\textbf{Q2--Q4. Do messages stay in a finite family?} Your family, a Gaussian pushed once through the chain,
$\normal{m}{v}$ dilated by $\corr$ and convolved with $\innov$, is exact at the first site and very close afterwards:
$\cmpAnsatzOverGauss\times$ smaller KL than a plain Gaussian and $78\times$ smaller than a 4-parameter Gaussian+Laplace at low
noise. But it is not closed, $(G*\innov)\cdot\mathcal N\neq G'*\innov$: the interior error is above the quadrature floor in 432 of
432 configurations, and does not move when the grid is doubled. The Gaussian closes; we have no proof about other families. \fals{We predicted the family would do
best at high noise; it does worst there,} because with a flat likelihood the recursion drives messages to the stationary law, an
infinite convolution.

\textbf{Q5. Fourier BP?} Two of the three operations are free in Fourier space, which gives the message in closed form,
$\varphi_{k+1}(\omega)=\varphi_\mu(\corr^k\omega)\prod_{j<k}\varphi_\varepsilon(\corr^j\omega)$ (checked against grid BP to
$2\times10^{-8}$). The likelihood is the hard step, a convolution in $\omega$ of width $e^{-t}/\sqrt{\Dt}$, widest at low noise.
As a representation, Fourier modes on a window adapted to each message need $\cmpFourierAdaptedLo$ numbers (KL $10^{-2}$) or
$\cmpFourierAdaptedHi$ ($10^{-4}$) against the grid's $\cmpGridLo$ and $\cmpGridHi$, close to your ``20 modes''; but they reach
$10^{-4}$ in only \cmpFourierReach{} cases and adding modes can make it worse, so a solver cannot choose a truncation. Cumulant
(Gram--Charlier) truncation does not converge: Cramér's condition fails for every message, and adding cumulants makes the
reconstruction worse in 144 of 144 cases. And the transition step is only
$\cmpMatmulShareLo$--$\cmpMatmulShareHi$ of a sweep, so making it free buys at most $\cmpFftCapLo$--$\cmpFftCapHi\times$; our FFT
version is $\cmpFftSlower\times$ slower at $401$ points. The coarse grid is the better trick (Section~\ref{sec:predictions}).

\textbf{Q6. A neural network for the kernel?} It trains through BP and loses where a small kernel is right: on the linear chain
the 1{,}516-parameter MDN is worse than the 25-number mixture in 64 of 64 comparisons ($2.1$--$2.9\times$) and worse than the best
plain score network. \fals{We predicted it would sit between the two.} It wins where the simple family is wrong
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). This is the overfitting you predicted in the call.

\textbf{Q7. Time per posterior mean?} See Section~\ref{sec:honest}: BP is slower ($\cmpBpMsEightThreads$ against
$\cmpTfMsEightThreads$\,ms, eight threads; on the H200 $15\times$ at batch 512, $6\times$ at 2048, because BP is latency-bound).
What scales well is training: the EM M-step costs the same whatever $\nseq$ (the E-step is linear in $\nseq$), because the kernel
is evaluated on the grid only.

\textbf{Q8. Generation without a score?} Yes. On a chain, sampling site by site from the kernel is exact, and it is
$\genAncestralCostRatio\times$ cheaper than a 200-step reverse SDE at equal error (eight threads, 2{,}048 sequences). A Gaussian score generates Gaussian samples
(excess kurtosis of $a_{i+1}-0.85a_i$ about $0$ against $1.92$).

\textbf{Q9. Fixed $t$ or pooled?} Per level: EM--BP beats every network at all twelve levels, and beats the Gaussian oracle at
the $\lvlFirstLevelsA$ ($\nseq=128$) or $\lvlFirstLevelsB$ ($\nseq=2048$) lowest. Above that the non-Gaussian structure left is
smaller than the kernel's estimation error; the crossover moves from $t\approx1$ to $t\approx2$ with 16 times more data.
\fals{We predicted first place at every level.}

\textbf{Q10. Information about $\corr,\beta$ or about $\kappa_2,\kappa_4$?} Recomputed, not relabelled. With $\kappa_2=q$,
$\kappa_4=\gamma_2(\beta)q^2$ and $J=\partial(\corr,\kappa_2,\kappa_4)/\partial(\corr,q,\beta)$, the efficiency of coordinate $k$ is
$1/(j_k^\top I_\params^{-1}j_k)$; a relabel would be wrong by $1.6$--$1526\times$. The message survives: fourth-order information
falls about $700\times$ more than second order from $t=0.05$ to $t=3$. Following your advice it is a supporting result, not
the headline.

\textbf{Q11. Scaling in $\nseq$ and $\nsites$?} The call's answer was wrong. A sweep is $O(\nsites\,\ngrid^2 B)$ operations for $B$
sequences: measured slopes $\cmpSlopeBatch$ in $B$, $\cmpSlopeLength$ in $\nsites$, and $\cmpSlopeGrid$ (not $2$) in $\ngrid$,
because element-wise work and memory traffic dominate the matrix product. Memory: about $\cmpMemMb$\,MB for 512 sequences of 32
sites, $\cmpMemRatioMin$--$\cmpMemRatioMax$ times the largest network.

plot: re-plotted in cumulant coordinates. Notation: one table; ``driving noise'' instead of ``innovation'', ``linear chain''
instead of AR(1). Clean-input network arm: dropped.
